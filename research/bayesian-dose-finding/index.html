Biostatistics / Bayesian dose finding

Xinzhu Wang · Tanzy Love · Unpublished manuscript

Bayesian Optimization for Dose Finding with Two Agents: Participant Allocation and Final Selection

Advisor: Tanzy Love (University of Rochester).

Dual-agent dose finding must balance efficacy and tolerability, both when assigning the next cohort and when recommending a final dose combination. Motivated by MARIPOSA’s results in obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), I developed a constrained knowledge-gradient evaluator to assess how another cohort could improve the final choice. My simulations evaluate participant allocation and final selection separately.

This page presents selected simulation findings. The full manuscript, derivations and research software are not publicly available; release is subject to advisor approval.

Clinical motivation: MARIPOSA and OSA

Two decisions in dose-finding research

Bayesian optimization uses a response model and acquisition rule to choose what to test next. Final selection uses the accumulated evidence to recommend a combination.

My project studies these statistical decisions through simulation. It is not a report of running MARIPOSA or a complete clinical trial design.

Simulation results

Simulation findings · Not clinical trial outcomes

Efficacy gains came with toxicity trade-offs

In the OSA-derived full-grid simulation at posterior cutoff 0.70, cKG versus cEI increased final-selection efficacy by 0.91 simulation units (95% Monte Carlo interval: 0.51 to 1.31). Above-limit final selection increased by 9.75 percentage points (5.18 to 14.32), and above-limit post-initialization allocation by 2.33 points (0.69 to 3.98). These paired contrasts average two strata, retain fallback selections and use pointwise unadjusted intervals.

Allocation and final selection can diverge

Across the four synthetic scenario summaries, cKG allocated above the limit less often than tMSE, yet selected above-limit final doses more often.

Rankings changed with toxicity assumptions

Comparisons with entropy changed under different assumptions about toxicity variability. A single ranking did not hold across those assumptions.

These comparisons show why participant allocation and final selection need separate evaluation, with efficacy and toxicity reported for each.

Scope. Exploratory simulations concern mean toxicity, not patient adverse-event rates. They do not establish a clinically deployable dose-finding design.

Acquisition ideas and their mathematics

cKG · General look-ahead idea

αcKG(q)=𝔼[V+(q)|D]−V

General expected-value identity; the research-specific value function and evaluator are withheld.

cEI · Expected efficacy improvement

αcEI(q)=pq[ΔqΦ(ze,q)+σe,qφ(ze,q)]

Weight expected efficacy improvement by the probability that mean toxicity is below the limit.

tMSE · Uncertainty near the mean-toxicity limit

αtMSE(q)=σg,qexp(−zg,q22)

Prioritize toxicity uncertainty close to the specified limit.

Entropy · Uncertainty about qualifying dose pairs

αent(q)=H−𝔼[H+(q)|D]

H = Σᵢ h(pᵢ) sums marginal entropies, not joint entropy; h(p) = −p log p − (1−p) log(1−p).

Shared notation and the sequential update
pq=Φ(zg,q),zg,q=L−μg,qσg,q,pq>τ

μ and σ are latent posterior means and SDs. Φ and φ are the standard-normal CDF and density. For cEI, Δq = μe,q − e⋆ and ze,q = Δq/σe,q. In this teaching example, e⋆ is the greatest predicted efficacy among qualifying pairs. With none, allocation pauses and cEI is not evaluated. V denotes a chosen decision value in the general look-ahead identity; its research-specific definition is withheld. A superscript + denotes updating with one possible observation.

For response channel r, the next two-person cohort mean is drawn from N(μr,q, vr,q), where vr,q = Σr,qq + η²r/2. With ki = Σr,iq, Gaussian conditioning gives:

μ+=μ+kv(Y¯−μq),Σ+=Σ−kkTv

Both channels are updated independently. The three implemented teaching rules use the same strict probability criterion. The illustration keeps model parameters fixed and computes the entropy expectation numerically. The cKG card states a general principle only; it is not an implementation of the manuscript.

Teaching example: assumptions and scope

Independent Gaussian processes model synthetic efficacy and continuous toxicity, with fixed parameters and Gaussian noise. Both cohort outcomes arrive before the next assignment; delays, missing data and accrual are omitted.

All 25 standardized pairs are available and may be revisited; zero denotes the lowest dose, not necessarily placebo. An unobserved pair may have the best qualifying prediction. These simplified rules do not reproduce the manuscript or MARIPOSA.

The cKG implementation and derivation remain withheld. These teaching controls are not clinically validated; the probability threshold concerns mean toxicity, not a patient’s dose-limiting-toxicity risk or a safety guarantee.
