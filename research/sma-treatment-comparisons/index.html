Clinical research / SMA treatment comparisons

Motor outcomes during nusinersen and risdiplam treatment in spinal muscular atrophy: an APNCR registry study

Advisors: Abdus S. Wahed (University of Rochester); Divya Jayaraman (Department of Neurology, Columbia University).

Differences in motor scores can reflect who receives each drug and who returns for assessment. I updated the APNCR registry comparison and added upper-limb function, using the same four-model framework for HFMSE and RULM while retaining eligible patients without follow-up in each outcome’s reference population.

Selected manuscript findings · Updated October 2026 · Records through August 11, 2026. The manuscript and underlying patient data remain unreleased; their public release is subject to advisor approval.

Clinical context and motor-function outcomes

This observational study compares nusinersen and risdiplam in SMA types II and III. Treatment was not randomly assigned.

Nusinersen

Intrathecal administration

Given into the cerebrospinal fluid by lumbar puncture.

Risdiplam

Oral administration

Taken by mouth.

The Hammersmith Functional Motor Scale Expanded (HFMSE, 0–66) assesses gross motor function; the Revised Upper Limb Module (RULM, 0–37) assesses upper-limb function. Higher scores indicate better function. Each outcome has its own first eligible complete assessment during treatment as baseline.

CHOP INTEND could not support the planned adjusted comparison: only seven patients in the SMA II/III cohort had eligible follow-up, including one risdiplam patient with one score.

The reference cohorts contain 151 HFMSE patients and 150 RULM patients; these outcome-specific cohorts overlap and should not be added together. For each outcome, 115 patients contributed follow-up. The other 36 HFMSE and 35 RULM patients remain in propensity-score estimation and the reference average but contribute no postbaseline outcome records to model fitting.

Incomplete assessments—23 HFMSE and eight RULM—are excluded from score summaries and model fitting. An incomplete assessment does not by itself remove an otherwise eligible patient.

Follow-up limits and outcome-specific baselines

Both outcomes use a common cap of 42 calendar months from original treatment initiation. Each patient’s endpoint is the earliest of that cap, a recorded switch, a recorded death or August 11, 2026. Switch-day and death-day scores are excluded. A later baseline leaves a shorter postbaseline window, and the last observed assessment may occur before the endpoint.

The common cap supports consistent comparisons; it is not proven optimal for either outcome. Later follow-up has fewer treatment-group comparisons. No patient is assumed to have continuous or complete 42-month follow-up.

Why a 42-month limit?

The analysis uses the same pragmatic treatment-duration cap for HFMSE and RULM. Later comparisons have sparse risdiplam follow-up, and support differs between outcomes; 42 months is not a proven optimal limit for either outcome.

The cap was not chosen by treatment-effect significance. It does not mean that every patient was observed for 42 months, and it does not define a biological threshold. Actual eligibility uses calendar dates, and missing assessments are not filled in.

These are fully adjusted, overlap-weighted risdiplam − nusinersen average score differences. HFMSE numerically favors risdiplam; the adjusted RULM difference is close to zero. Both intervals include zero, so these data establish neither superiority nor equivalence.

Each estimate averages fitted score differences over each patient’s baseline-to-endpoint period, then equally across the full outcome-specific baseline cohort. It is not a month-42 contrast, an individual patient’s change or a difference in rates of improvement. The four models for each outcome use the same eligible follow-up records and the same reference average.

The fully adjusted models include baseline HFMSE, age, sex, SMA type, SMN2 category, treatment-start year and treatment duration before baseline. “Treatment only” models have no fixed time terms; their fitted group difference is constant across the averaging period.

Analysis methods

  1. Make the treatment groups more comparable. Because treatment was not randomized and the smaller risdiplam group differs at baseline, propensity-score overlap weighting gives more weight to patients whose recorded characteristics are compatible with either drug, without discarding patients through matching. A model estimates each patient’s probability of receiving risdiplam: the risdiplam weight is one minus that probability, and the nusinersen weight is the probability itself. These are treatment weights, not inverse-probability-of-censoring weights (IPCW).
  2. Use available repeated scores, with explicit missingness assumptions. A patient random intercept accounts for dependence between that patient’s assessments. Missing follow-up is not filled in or treated as no change. Valid interpretation still depends on the outcome model and on missingness being explainable by observed information included in the analysis; treatment weighting does not correct informative dropout or unmeasured confounding.
  3. Compare the same four model specifications. All four are linear mixed-effects models of follow-up HFMSE or RULM scores. The two reference models include treatment only, with and without overlap weights. The fully adjusted pair adds time since the outcome-specific baseline, treatment × time and seven baseline covariates, again with and without weights. Time is a fixed slope in these adjusted models; the random effect is the patient intercept, with no random time slope.

Baseline adjustment: baseline HFMSE or RULM, age, sex, SMA type, SMN2 category, treatment-start year and treatment duration before baseline. The propensity-score model uses the same baseline information. Treatment-start year captures calendar-era differences; prior treatment duration captures exposure before baseline; follow-up time describes the subsequent observation period.

We used 5,000 whole-patient bootstrap samples for each model’s 95% confidence interval, keeping repeated assessments together. Re-estimating treatment probabilities in weighted samples also captures uncertainty in the estimated weights.

Weight implementation and numerical checks

Each patient’s overlap weight multiplies that patient’s joint log likelihood after integrating out the random intercept. The weights are not residual-precision weights. Model fitting uses these overlap weights; the final predicted comparison averages patients equally over the full baseline reference cohort, so it can still depend on extrapolation where treatment groups have little overlap.

Bootstrap resampling is stratified by treatment at the patient level, with eligibility reapplied and the same draw used for all four models within an outcome. All 5,000 fits returned for every HFMSE and RULM model. Valid fits that estimated no additional patient-intercept variance were retained, including 15 HFMSE and 29 RULM fits in the fully adjusted weighted analyses. Numerical failures are recorded rather than replaced; none occurred for these two outcomes.

The 95% intervals are percentile intervals for each model, not simultaneous guarantees across models. They describe sampling uncertainty under the analysis assumptions and do not account for every possible source of bias.

Patient characteristics can confound the comparison.

Age, prior disease severity and treatment history can influence both which drug a patient receives and later motor function. A raw difference can therefore reflect prognosis as well as treatment.

Interpretation and limitations

RULM precision is limited by only 12 risdiplam patients contributing follow-up. Its 314 repeated scores came from 115 people, not 314 independent patients. A near-zero adjusted estimate is compatible with no average difference, but its interval does not establish equivalence; a negative estimate does not mean patients declined.

HFMSE and RULM measure different aspects of motor function and need not show the same pattern. Both comparisons remain limited by the small risdiplam follow-up groups, differences in treatment-start years, missing assessments and possible unmeasured confounding.

Baseline often occurs after treatment has begun, so the adjusted comparison describes subsequent function conditional on baseline function and treatment history. It does not measure total benefit from treatment initiation. Original treatment classifications are retained, but post-switch outcomes are excluded; this is not a formal intention-to-treat analysis.
