Clinical research / SMA treatment comparisons

Motor outcomes during nusinersen and risdiplam treatment in spinal muscular atrophy: an APNCR registry study

Advisors: Abdus S. Wahed (University of Rochester); Divya Jayaraman (Department of Neurology, Columbia University).

Differences in motor scores can reflect who receives each drug and who is assessed. I applied established adjustment and sensitivity methods to APNCR registry data, retaining patients without complete follow-up and using partial scores as bounds rather than imputed totals.

Selected manuscript findings · Updated September 2026. The manuscript and underlying patient data remain unreleased; public release is subject to advisor approval.

Clinical context and motor-function outcome

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) causes progressive muscle weakness. Nusinersen and risdiplam both increase production of survival motor neuron protein, but are administered differently. This observational study concerns patients with SMA types II and III.

Intrathecal administration

Risdiplam

Oral administration

Taken by mouth.

The Hammersmith Functional Motor Scale Expanded (HFMSE) scores 33 motor tasks from 0 to 2, giving a total of 0–66; higher scores indicate better function. Baseline is the first complete assessment during treatment, which may already reflect drug exposure.

What counts as a meaningful score change?

The study uses a gain of at least 3 HFMSE points as a descriptive within-person criterion, adapted from CHERISH’s month-15 secondary outcome. It is not a validated threshold for this mixed-age cohort or an equivalence margin between drugs. Patients without an assessment are not counted as nonresponders.

Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) concerns the smallest change patients perceive as important in a specified clinical context. Minimal detectable change (MDC) concerns change beyond measurement error at a specified confidence level. Neither is a test of statistical significance. HFMSE floor and ceiling limits also affect how much an individual score can change.

Adjusted treatment comparison

+0.88 HFMSE pointsRisdiplam − nusinersen · 95% patient-bootstrap interval: −3.18 to +4.79

Relative motor benefit remains unresolved. The point estimate is slightly positive, but the interval permits differences in either direction. These data establish neither superiority nor equivalence.

The comparison averages fitted differences across each patient’s retained treatment period, then equally across all 159 eligible baseline patients—including those without a complete follow-up score. It is not a month-42 contrast or a total causal effect from treatment initiation.

Models, adjustment and the comparison target

Fractional-logit mean (S2): fits mean HFMSE divided by 66 with a logistic link, then returns predictions to the 0–66 score scale. It does not model an improvement probability or assume 66 independent trials.

Full linear change (S3): includes time, treatment × time and covariate × time terms for baseline HFMSE, age and prior treatment duration. Simple linear change (S4): retains time and treatment × time, with the same treatment and assessment weights. It is not an unweighted comparison or a model forcing the nusinersen trajectory to be flat.

Coefficients are re-estimated in each model. Both linear change models pass through zero at baseline; the primary model uses baseline as a covariate without forcing its fitted value. Score bounds alone do not ensure better prediction or less bias.

Adjustment includes age, sex, SMA type, SMN2, baseline HFMSE, treatment-start year and the treatment-to-baseline interval. Outcome fitting combines treatment-overlap weights with inverse assessment-intensity weights based on recorded history. There is no inverse no-switch factor. These fitting weights differ from the equal patient weights used for the final average.

Bootstrap resampling keeps each patient’s repeated records together and refits the analysis. These intervals are pointwise, not simultaneous guarantees across models or sensitivity settings. They reflect sampling uncertainty under the specified analysis; they do not absorb unmeasured confounding or uncertainty from post-hoc model choices, or guarantee exact 95% coverage in this sparse sample.

Recording assumptions change both the size and direction of the estimate. All 25 intervals include zero. This does not establish stable effect sizes or robustness to every missingness mechanism.

What the assumptions mean

A ratio of 2 compares, for example, current scores of 40 and 30 at the same time and recorded history: the complete-score recording rate is assumed to be twice as high. It describes assessment intensity, not a visit probability, drug effect or within-person improvement. Ten points is a scaling unit, not a clinical threshold.

A ratio of 1 removes only additional current-score dependence; it does not imply equal assessment rates across treatments. A complete record requires both assessment completion and recording. These scenarios do not cover permanent loss of observation opportunity.

Each setting refits the observation and outcome models and uses 1,000 patient-bootstrap samples. At ratio 1 in both groups, the estimate is +0.88 with interval −3.08 to +4.83; this separate run does not replace the main 5,000-sample interval of −3.18 to +4.79. The analysis adapts the Yiu–Su selection-function approach to a parametric recording-rate model.

Patient characteristics can confound the comparison.

Age, prior disease severity and treatment history can influence both which drug a patient receives and later motor function. A raw difference can therefore reflect prognosis as well as treatment.

A causal interpretation of the drug comparison would require well-defined treatment strategies, consistency, exchangeability and positivity. Better scoring bounds and observation models do not by themselves establish those conditions.

Interpretation and limitations

Only 13 risdiplam patients contributed complete follow-up scores, and treatment-start years had limited overlap. Retaining everyone in the comparison does not recover their unobserved outcomes or remove unmeasured confounding.

Additional model checks

Adding age/type-specific slopes or treatment-specific curvature gave +0.90 and +0.63 points; both bootstrap intervals included zero. These check trajectory-shape assumptions, not unmeasured confounding.

How the retained treatment period is defined

Each period ends at the earliest recorded switch, recorded death, treatment initiation plus 42 calendar months, or the working data cutoff of February 6, 2025. It must have positive duration after baseline. Switch-day and death-day scores are excluded because within-day ordering is unknown. The first recorded period is not verified lifetime first-line or uninterrupted treatment.

Why a 42-month limit?

The most populated six-month duration interval among all 118 recorded risdiplam starters was >36–42 months. This population included prior nusinersen recipients and people without a complete HFMSE baseline. The rule used follow-up durations, not treatment-effect estimates.

The observed duration distribution selected an operational limit. It does not imply continuous observation for 42 months or define a biological threshold.
